> [!abstract] Overview Stress and anxiety involve communication between different brain regions and the nervous system. These systems help us recognize potential threats and prepare the body to respond.
>
> Understanding them helps explain why we experience nervousness, fear, increased heart rate, and difficulty concentrating.

## 1. What is stress?

Stress is the body's response to situations perceived as challenging, demanding, or threatening.

Examples:

- Preparing for an exam.
- Speaking in front of an audience.
- Experiencing conflict.
- Facing an unfamiliar situation.

Stress can be helpful in certain situations because it increases alertness and prepares us to act.

However, prolonged or excessive stress can negatively affect physical and psychological health.

## 2. What happens in the brain during stress?

Several brain structures work together to process stressful situations.

**Amygdala**

- Processes emotional significance and potential threats.
- Contributes to fear and emotional responses.

**Hippocampus**

- Helps form memories and understand the context of situations.
- Helps distinguish between safe and threatening environments.

**Prefrontal cortex**

- Involved in decision-making, attention, planning, and emotional regulation.
- Helps evaluate situations and regulate responses.
- It compare the situation to past memories to see if you are actually safe.

**Hypothalamus**

- Coordinates important physiological stress responses.
- Activates the sympathetic nervous system and the hormonal stress response.

## 3. The fight-or-flight response

When the brain detects a potential threat, the sympathetic nervous system can activate the body's fight-or-flight response.

This prepares the body to respond quickly.

Common effects include:

- Increased heart rate.
- Faster breathing.
- Sweating.
- Increased alertness.
- Muscle tension.
- Reduced digestive activity.

### What hormones are involved?

**Adrenaline (epinephrine)**

- Released by the adrenal glands.
- Increases heart rate and prepares the body for immediate action.

**Cortisol**

- A hormone released through activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.
- Helps regulate the body's response to prolonged demands and stress.

### The HPA axis

The stress hormone pathway involves three main structures:

1. Hypothalamus releases CRH.
2. Pituitary gland releases ACTH.
3. Adrenal cortex releases cortisol.

This system helps regulate the body's hormonal response to stress.

## 4. What is anxiety?

Anxiety is a state of apprehension, worry, or fear about a possible future event.

Unlike an immediate fear response to a present threat, anxiety often involves anticipation of something that might happen.

**Example: Public speaking**

Imagine you have to present in front of 100 people tomorrow.

You might:

- Worry about making mistakes.
- Imagine people judging you.
- Experience a racing heart.
- Have difficulty sleeping.
- Feel nervous before the presentation begins.

Your brain is responding to an anticipated situation, even though the presentation has not happened yet.

## 5. Acute stress vs chronic stress

|Acute stress|Chronic stress|
|---|---|
|Short-term response|Prolonged or repeated response|
|Can improve alertness|Can interfere with normal functioning|
|Helps prepare for immediate challenges|Can affect sleep, mood and concentration|
|Usually resolves after the situation|May require changes and support to manage|

Prolonged stress can affect brain functions involved in memory, emotional regulation, and attention.

## 6. How can we regulate stress?

Some strategies that can help manage stress include:

- Deep, controlled breathing.
- Regular physical activity.
- Adequate sleep.
- Mindfulness and meditation.
- Social support.
- Planning and breaking difficult tasks into smaller steps.
- Seeking professional support when needed.

These strategies can help regulate stress responses and support psychological well-being.

## 7. How does neuroplasticity relate to anxiety?

The brain learns from repeated experiences.

For example, someone who repeatedly avoids public speaking may continue associating it with danger or discomfort.

Gradual exposure and practice can help the brain learn new responses.

Over time, experiences of successfully managing presentations can modify fear-related learning and help reduce anxiety.

This demonstrates how [[Neuroplasticity]] contributes to emotional learning and adaptation.

## 8. Key takeaways

> [!important]
>
> - Stress is the body's response to challenging or threatening situations.
> - The amygdala contributes to processing potential threats.
> - The prefrontal cortex helps with emotional regulation and decision-making.
> - The hypothalamus coordinates important stress responses.
> - The sympathetic nervous system activates fight-or-flight responses.
> - Adrenaline and cortisol are important stress-related hormones.
> - Chronic stress can negatively affect memory, mood, and concentration.
> - Neuroplasticity allows the brain to learn and modify responses to stressful situations.
